East Africa Market Knowledge

Real problems exporters face - and what they cost.

1. The Non-Tariff Barrier Problem

The East African Community now comprises eight countries - Burundi, DR Congo, Kenya, Rwanda, Somalia, South Sudan, Tanzania and Uganda. Its customs union promises free movement of goods.

In practice, trade between member states remains stuck at around 15 percent of their total trade. The reason is non-tariff barriers - surprise levies, permit delays, extra inspections, duplicated paperwork and road user charges.

Who is responsible

Figures from the EAC Regional Monitoring Committee show the scale of the issue. A deadline of 30 June 2026 for eliminating these barriers passed without resolution.

What this looks like in practice

  • In February 2024 Tanzania halted new tea import permits for Kenyan traders, citing quality concerns.
  • Border blockades and permit delays occur at crossings such as Namanga between Kenya and Tanzania.
  • Ugandan dairy processors began pursuing Nigeria instead, rather than regional neighbours.

Presidents Ruto and Samia Suluhu committed during a May state visit to eliminating all barriers between Kenya and Tanzania by mid-2026. Implementation remains to be seen.

Practical point for exporters: goods moving between EAC countries cannot be assumed to move freely. Each border has its own realities.

2. Documentation - The Hidden Cost

Kenya has digitised many procedures through its National Electronic Single Window System. Exporters are nonetheless required to obtain multiple documents before goods move.

Typical documents required

  • Certificate of origin
  • Phytosanitary certificate
  • Export health certificate
  • Customs declaration
  • Commercial invoice
  • Transport documents
  • Insurance certificate
  • Laboratory testing certificates or additional regulatory approvals, depending on product

Documentation costs for a typical export consignment are estimated at KSh 15,000 to 30,000, excluding freight and taxes.

Individually each requirement seems manageable. Cumulatively, the administrative burden falls hardest on smaller exporters with limited staff and working capital.

3. Clearance Delays and Unpredictability

Delay is not the real problem. Unpredictability is.

  • At the Nakonde-Tunduma border between Tanzania and Zambia, roughly 30,000 customs declarations are processed monthly, with average clearance around 2.5 days. Delays are caused by scanner downtime, connectivity issues, documentation verification and congestion.
  • Transit times on the Nairobi to Lusaka route vary from 8 days to as long as 30 days.

For a manufacturer on a production schedule, a delivery window of 8 to 30 days is not a schedule at all. It ties up working capital and puts customer commitments at risk.

4. A Live Example - Mombasa, August 2026

The Kenya Revenue Authority introduced a new Export Declaration documentation requirement effective 3 August 2026.

Clearing agents reported a logjam of vehicles and transit cargo at the Port of Mombasa, container freight stations and inland container depots. Cargo shipped before the requirement took effect could not be cleared.

Importers and transporters became exposed to storage, demurrage and detention charges. Agents argued the requirement duplicated documentation already generated in the exporting country.

Mombasa handles cargo for Uganda, Rwanda, Burundi, South Sudan, eastern DR Congo and northern Tanzania. A single documentation change therefore affects six countries.

This is what non-tariff barriers actually look like. Not a published tariff, but a rule change that arrives without warning and costs money immediately.

5. Payment Fragmentation

A clearing agent in Nairobi tracking payment for a shipment to Kampala may have to navigate mobile money, bank transfers and informal payment channels at the same time - while hoping funds arrive before customs clearance deadlines.

Delays in payment translate directly into delays in cargo movement, and additional cost.

This is why buyer verification matters here. A buyer who intends to pay, but cannot move funds reliably, creates the same problem as one who never intended to pay.

6. The System Favours Large Exporters

African logistics systems tend to favour high-volume exporters. Large manufacturers negotiate lower freight rates and spread fixed documentation and compliance costs across many shipments.

A smaller exporter sending one or two containers carries the same compliance burden across far less volume.

This is precisely why first-time and smaller exporters need ground support most, and can least afford to learn by making mistakes.

7. Certification - The Most Common Costly Error

Regulated goods entering Kenya, Tanzania and Uganda require a Certificate of Conformity, issued in India before shipment, through inspection agencies appointed by the respective national standards bureaux - KEBS, TBS and UNBS.

Ship without it and Tanzania applies a penalty of 15 percent of CIF value. Consignments may be re-exported or destroyed at the exporter's cost.

Appointed agencies include Intertek, SGS, TUV Rheinland, Bureau Veritas and Cotecna.

Kenya additionally requires a Free Sale Certificate for certain high-risk categories, including food products, cosmetics, toys, electrical and electronic goods, and fertilizers.

8. What All of This Means

Every problem listed above shares one characteristic. None of it is visible from an office in India.

  • You cannot see a border blockade from a spreadsheet.
  • You cannot know a documentation rule changed last week unless someone tells you.
  • You cannot judge whether a buyer will pay by reading their company registration.
  • You cannot chase a stuck container by email.

Tariffs are visible and calculable.
Non-tariff barriers are invisible until you are standing there.
